<h2>Japan Shifts to AI-Centric Warfare</h2>
<p>Japan's Ministry of Defence is fundamentally rethinking its military strategy around artificial intelligence. Defense Minister <strong>Shinjiro Koizumi</strong> announced that the ministry will publish its <strong>"Defense AI Vision"</strong> by the end of November 2026 — a comprehensive strategic document that overhauls the 2024 AI policy and explicitly shifts Japan's defense posture from <strong>"network-centric warfare" to "AI and data-centric warfare."</strong></p>
<p>The move comes as Japan watches the US and China accelerate their military AI programs, with the ministry's own analysis concluding that <strong>"AI will determine the outcome of battles"</strong> in future conflicts.</p>

<h2>What the Defense AI Vision Changes</h2>
<p>The new policy represents a fundamental shift from Japan's 2024 AI guidelines, which focused on research and principles. The Defense AI Vision prioritizes <strong>operational deployment</strong>:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Air surveillance automation:</strong> AI will be incorporated into the Japan Air Self-Defense Force's automated air surveillance and control system by <strong>March 2027</strong> — less than 18 months away</li>
  <li><strong>Seven priority domains:</strong> Command and control, unmanned systems, logistics support, intelligence analysis, cybersecurity, training simulation, and administrative operations</li>
  <li><strong>Cloud migration for classified data:</strong> The ministry is moving classified defense data from isolated internal systems to secure cloud infrastructure, enabling AI-powered cross-service analysis for the first time</li>
  <li><strong>AI-assisted parliamentary operations:</strong> Since February 2026, the ministry has deployed a generative AI assistant for drafting parliamentary responses — an early example of AI in government administration</li>
</ul>

<h2>The Catalyst: Lessons from Modern Conflicts</h2>
<p>Japan's urgency stems from the ministry's analysis of recent conflicts. The Defense Ministry's Defense Power Transformation Headquarters concluded in August 2026 that:</p>
<ul>
  <li>A new form of warfare combining <strong>drones and AI</strong> in complex ways has taken root in modern conflicts</li>
  <li>The US military's use of AI for target selection and attack planning has <strong>dramatically improved decision-making speed</strong> and operational efficiency</li>
  <li>Japan's Self-Defense Forces risk falling behind the <strong>speed of US military decision-making</strong> without equivalent AI capabilities</li>
  <li>Japan's current satellite intelligence and command-and-control capabilities are <strong>"limited"</strong> without US military support — a vulnerability that AI-powered autonomous systems could address</li>
</ul>

<h2>The AI-First Military Team</h2>
<p>The ministry established a <strong>direct-report AI Implementation Team</strong> under the defense minister in January 2026. This team has already overseen several initiatives, and the Defense AI Vision will formalize its mandate with specific deployment timelines and budget allocations.</p>
<p>The ruling Liberal Democratic Party has separately prepared an <strong>"AI White Paper 2.0"</strong> calling for the fundamental transformation from network-centric to AI-centric operations, with recommendations to be reflected in the year-end revision of Japan's three key security documents and the national AI basic plan.</p>

<h2>Budget and Infrastructure</h2>
<p>The proposed defense AI budget is part of Japan's broader national AI push. The government's AI headquarters has acknowledged that while large enterprises and research institutions are rapidly adopting AI, Japan's overall AI development investment lags behind what its economic position warrants.</p>
<p>Key infrastructure investments include:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Classified data cloud migration:</strong> Budget requests for 2027 to move high-security defense data to AI-capable cloud platforms</li>
  <li><strong>Target detection systems:</strong> AI for automatic target identification, friend-or-foe recognition, and electromagnetic spectrum analysis</li>
  <li><strong>Open-source intelligence:</strong> Automated screening of social media, electromagnetic data, and other public information sources</li>
</ul>

<h2>Human Oversight: Japan's Red Line</h2>
<p>Despite the push toward AI automation, Japan's Defense AI Vision emphasizes <strong>human oversight</strong> as a non-negotiable principle. The ministry has consistently stressed that humans must remain in the decision-making loop for lethal force decisions — a position that aligns with Japan's constitutional constraints on military operations.</p>
<p>This human-in-the-loop requirement distinguishes Japan's approach from some other nations and reflects the broader debate about autonomous weapons systems that has intensified at the United Nations in recent years.</p>

<h2>Global Military AI Context</h2>
<p>Japan's move is part of a worldwide military AI acceleration:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>United States:</strong> The Pentagon renamed its digital AI office to "Super Intelligence Office" and issued an "AI First" directive for all military operations</li>
  <li><strong>China:</strong> Accelerating military AI with significant state investment and integration into joint operations</li>
  <li><strong>South Korea:</strong> Proposed defense AI budget of 361 billion won, with telecom and cloud firms competing for contracts — <a href="/en/blog/south-korea-record-ai-budget-6-7-billion-2026">read our coverage</a></li>
  <li><strong>EU:</strong> AI Gigafactories initiative with potential dual-use applications</li>
</ul>

<h2>What This Means for Japan's AI Industry</h2>
<p>Defense AI spending creates ripple effects throughout Japan's technology sector. Companies like Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (already developing command-and-control software), NEC, Fujitsu, and Hitachi are positioned to benefit from defense AI contracts. The ministry's cloud migration alone will drive significant demand for secure AI infrastructure.</p>
<p>For Japan's AI startup ecosystem — which produced 5 "notable" AI models in 2025, ranking third globally — defense applications represent a potentially transformative market.</p>

<h2>The Bottom Line</h2>
<p>Japan's Defense AI Vision marks the end of a cautious, research-first approach to military AI and the beginning of rapid operational deployment. With AI-powered air surveillance targeted for March 2027, classified data moving to AI-capable clouds, and a ruling party pushing for AI-centric warfare doctrine, Japan is making its most significant defense technology shift since the end of the Cold War. The question isn't whether Japan will have an AI-first military — it's whether the transformation will happen fast enough to keep pace with its strategic competitors.</p>
