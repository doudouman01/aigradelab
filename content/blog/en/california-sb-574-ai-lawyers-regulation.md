<h2>California Regulates How Lawyers Use AI</h2>

<p>California Governor Gavin Newsom has signed <strong>Senate Bill 574</strong>, a law that regulates how attorneys and arbitrators use generative AI in legal proceedings. The law takes effect on <strong>January 1, 2027</strong>, making California one of the first states to impose specific requirements on AI use in the legal profession.</p>

<p>The core requirement: lawyers must verify the accuracy of AI-generated outputs and disclose when AI tools were used in preparing court filings.</p>

<h2>What SB 574 Requires</h2>

<p>The law introduces three main obligations for legal professionals:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Verification:</strong> Attorneys must independently verify any AI-generated content before submitting it to a court. This includes checking citations, case law references, and factual claims produced by AI tools</li>
<li><strong>Disclosure:</strong> Legal professionals must disclose AI assistance in court documents. If an AI tool contributed to drafting a brief, motion, or filing, that fact must be stated</li>
<li><strong>Prohibition on delegation:</strong> Arbitrators cannot delegate decision-making authority to AI systems. Human judgment must remain central to dispute resolution</li>
</ul>

<h2>Why This Law Matters</h2>

<p>SB 574 is a direct response to a well-documented problem: AI hallucinations in legal work. Since ChatGPT's launch, multiple attorneys have been sanctioned for submitting AI-generated briefs containing fabricated case citations — cases that looked real but did not exist.</p>

<p>High-profile incidents include:</p>

<ul>
<li>A New York attorney fined for citing six fictitious cases generated by ChatGPT in a federal court filing</li>
<li>Multiple instances across US courts where AI-generated legal documents contained plausible but entirely invented case law</li>
<li>Growing concern that AI tools optimized for fluency rather than accuracy create a specific risk in legal contexts where precision is mandatory</li>
</ul>

<p>By requiring verification and disclosure, California's law places the responsibility squarely on the attorney — not the AI tool — to ensure accuracy.</p>

<h2>How It Affects Legal AI Tools</h2>

<p>The law does not ban AI use in legal practice. Attorneys can continue using tools like ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, or specialized legal AI platforms. What changes is the workflow:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Before SB 574:</strong> An attorney could use an AI tool to draft a brief and submit it without disclosing the AI assistance</li>
<li><strong>After SB 574:</strong> The same attorney must verify every citation and factual claim, then include a disclosure statement in the filing</li>
</ul>

<p>This creates a market opportunity for legal AI tools that prioritize accuracy and citation verification over raw speed. Tools that can demonstrate lower hallucination rates and reliable source attribution will have a competitive advantage in California's legal market.</p>

<h2>The Bigger Regulatory Picture</h2>

<p>California's SB 574 is part of a broader wave of profession-specific AI regulation:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>California</strong> now requires AI verification and disclosure for lawyers (SB 574, effective Jan 2027)</li>
<li><strong>New York</strong> has signed a five-year ban on AI chatbot toys aimed at children (<a href="/en/blog/new-york-bans-ai-chatbot-toys">read our coverage</a>)</li>
<li><strong>The EU AI Act</strong> imposes transparency requirements for AI systems used in legal and judicial contexts</li>
<li><strong>Several US bar associations</strong> have issued guidance requiring attorneys to disclose AI use and verify outputs</li>
</ul>

<p>The pattern is clear: regulation is not targeting AI use broadly, but focusing on high-stakes domains — law, healthcare, finance, children's safety — where errors carry serious consequences.</p>

<h2>The Bottom Line</h2>

<p>SB 574 does not restrict lawyers from using AI. It requires them to use AI responsibly — by verifying outputs and being transparent about it. For the legal AI market, this is ultimately a quality signal: the tools that help attorneys meet these requirements will win.</p>
