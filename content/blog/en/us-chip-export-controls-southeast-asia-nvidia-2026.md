<h2>The AI Chip Map Is Being Redrawn</h2>
<p>The United States is dramatically tightening its control over who gets access to advanced AI chips — and the reverberations are reshaping the global AI landscape. In a series of moves in November 2026, two major developments have converged:</p>
<ul>
  <li>The <strong>Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS)</strong> has designated <strong>five countries</strong> — Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil — as having an <strong>"unacceptable risk of diversion"</strong> for advanced AI chips, requiring export licenses for shipments</li>
  <li><strong>Nvidia has completely stopped shipping</strong> licensed Hopper data center chips to China, despite having previously received US government approval for those sales</li>
</ul>
<p>Together, these actions represent the most aggressive expansion of AI chip export controls since the original restrictions began in 2022.</p>

<h2>The Remote Access Loophole — Now Closed</h2>
<p>The BIS rule, issued on <strong>November 15, 2026</strong>, targets a specific vulnerability in previous export controls: <strong>remote access to AI compute</strong>.</p>
<p>Previously, US authorities focused on blocking physical chip shipments at customs borders. But Chinese AI developers found a workaround — they could <strong>rent computing time</strong> on advanced Nvidia GPUs housed in data centers in Southeast Asian countries, effectively accessing restricted hardware without importing it.</p>
<p>The new rule closes this loophole by requiring export licenses for anyone seeking to ship advanced chips to the five flagged countries, and by extending controls to cover remote computing access from those locations. BIS described the change as a <strong>"logical extension"</strong> of existing requirements.</p>

<h2>Nvidia's China Shutdown</h2>
<p>Nvidia's interim CEO <strong>Colette Kress</strong> (who replaced Jensen Huang following his resignation) confirmed during the November 17 earnings call that Nvidia is no longer shipping licensed Hopper data center products to China:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Last quarter:</strong> Nvidia sold $2.4 billion worth of licensed Hopper chips to China</li>
  <li><strong>Going forward:</strong> "Our guidance does not have any licensed Hopper data center products included, and we are no longer shipping licensed Hopper data center products to China"</li>
  <li><strong>Approximately 600,000 H200 chips</strong> are reportedly being stored in Hong Kong awaiting Chinese approval that may never come</li>
  <li>Beijing has restricted H200 imports as it seeks to <strong>reduce dependence on US technology</strong></li>
</ul>
<p>China has also publicly questioned Nvidia chip purchases, with state media and cyberspace authorities raising alarms about reliance on American AI hardware.</p>

<h2>Chinese AI Firms Scramble for Alternatives</h2>
<p>Chinese AI companies including <strong>Moonshot AI, ByteDance, Alibaba, and Tencent</strong> are reportedly pursuing access to advanced Nvidia compute through overseas data centers as the new rules close the remote-access loophole. Options are narrowing:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Domestic chip development:</strong> Huawei is teasing next-generation AI memory technology, and other Chinese chipmakers are accelerating development of competitive alternatives</li>
  <li><strong>Efficiency innovation:</strong> Chinese AI labs have consistently found ways to do more with less compute — DeepSeek and other models have demonstrated competitive performance on restricted hardware</li>
  <li><strong>Alternative supply chains:</strong> Some Chinese firms are exploring chip access through countries not covered by the new restrictions</li>
</ul>

<h2>The Nvidia Leadership Crisis</h2>
<p>The export control story is inseparable from Nvidia's leadership upheaval. <strong>Jensen Huang resigned as CEO</strong> in November 2026 following personal scandals, with <strong>Colette Kress</strong> taking over as interim CEO. Huang had been the loudest advocate for continued chip exports to China — his departure removes the strongest internal voice for maintaining that business relationship.</p>

<h2>Global AI Competition Implications</h2>
<p>The export controls have implications far beyond chip sales:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>AI development speed:</strong> Chinese AI development could become slower and more expensive without access to cutting-edge hardware, potentially widening the US-China AI gap</li>
  <li><strong>Innovation pressure:</strong> Restricted access historically accelerates domestic innovation — China's semiconductor industry may emerge stronger in the long term</li>
  <li><strong>Market fragmentation:</strong> The global AI ecosystem is splitting into US-allied and China-aligned technology stacks, with Southeast Asia caught in between</li>
  <li><strong>Sovereign AI acceleration:</strong> Countries affected by export controls are investing more heavily in domestic AI infrastructure — <a href="/en/blog/canada-ai-for-all-strategy-budget-2026">Canada's $2.7B AI strategy</a> and <a href="/en/blog/south-korea-record-ai-budget-6-7-billion-2026">South Korea's $6.7B budget</a> reflect this trend</li>
</ul>

For the global AI industry, the message is unmistakable: where your chips are matters as much as what your models can do.</p>
