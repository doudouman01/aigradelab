<h2>Italy Moves First on the EU AI Act</h2>

<p>While most EU member states are still drafting their approach to the European AI Act, <strong>Italy has already made it operational</strong>. On June 10, 2026, the Italian government approved two implementing decrees that establish the governance structure, designate the responsible authorities, and set enforcement procedures for AI regulation in Italy.</p>

<p>This makes Italy the <strong>first EU country</strong> to have a functioning national AI governance framework under the AI Act — a significant milestone in the global race to regulate artificial intelligence.</p>

<h2>How Italy's AI Governance Works</h2>

<p>The two decrees establish a clear division of responsibilities among three existing agencies:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>AgID</strong> (Agenzia per l'Italia Digitale) — Responsible for AI standards, interoperability, and technical guidelines for public and private sector AI deployments</li>
<li><strong>ACN</strong> (Agenzia per la Cybersicurezza Nazionale) — Handles cybersecurity aspects of AI systems, including risk assessment for high-risk AI applications</li>
<li><strong>Garante per la Protezione dei Dati Personali</strong> — Oversees data protection compliance for AI systems, building on Italy's existing GDPR enforcement infrastructure</li>
</ul>

<p>Rather than creating a new AI-specific agency, Italy chose to distribute AI oversight across agencies that already have the expertise, staff, and legal authority to act — a pragmatic approach that allows faster implementation.</p>

<h2>What the AI Act Requires</h2>

<p>The EU AI Act, which entered into force in August 2024, categorizes AI systems by risk level:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Unacceptable risk:</strong> Banned outright (social scoring, real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces with limited exceptions)</li>
<li><strong>High risk:</strong> Subject to strict requirements — conformity assessments, documentation, human oversight, and registration in an EU database (examples: AI in hiring, credit scoring, law enforcement, medical devices)</li>
<li><strong>Limited risk:</strong> Transparency obligations — users must be told they are interacting with AI (chatbots, deepfakes)</li>
<li><strong>Minimal risk:</strong> No specific obligations (spam filters, AI in games)</li>
</ul>

<p>Member states must designate national authorities and establish enforcement mechanisms by August 2025. Italy did it early.</p>

<h2>Why Italy's Lead Matters</h2>

<p>Italy's early implementation has several implications:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Regulatory clarity for businesses:</strong> Companies operating in Italy now know who to contact, what to file, and what standards to meet — while businesses in other EU countries are still waiting for guidance</li>
<li><strong>Template for other countries:</strong> Italy's three-agency model is being studied by other EU member states as a potential template</li>
<li><strong>Competitive advantage:</strong> Italian AI companies and their international partners have a head start in building compliant products</li>
</ul>

<p>The regulatory wave is not limited to Europe. California recently signed <a href="/en/blog/california-sb-574-ai-lawyers-regulation">SB 574</a>, requiring lawyers to verify and disclose AI use, and <a href="/en/blog/new-york-bans-ai-chatbot-toys">New York banned AI chatbot toys</a> for children. The global direction is clear: regulation is coming, and early movers gain an advantage.</p>

<h2>The Bottom Line</h2>

<p>Italy is proving that implementing the AI Act does not require building new bureaucracy from scratch. By assigning responsibilities to existing agencies with relevant expertise, Italy created a governance model that is both fast and functional. For the rest of Europe, the question is no longer whether to implement — it is how quickly they can follow Italy's lead.</p>
